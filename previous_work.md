# Previous work

This note records the work closest to FuturePrefs, checked against the papers on 12 October 2026. It is organized by the questions the benchmark is trying not to repeat: utility and social-welfare elicitation, political and moral surveys, stated versus revealed preferences, time preference, existential-risk evaluations, and digital minds or future generations.

The gap is narrow. Models have already been given pairwise choices, and some of those choices have already been turned into utilities, exchange rates, and indifference points. What did not turn up is a benchmark that estimates indifference points for the six long-run civilizational tradeoffs used here: extinction risk against a stipulated upside, biological humans against digital successors, human control against welfare, present against future generations at civilizational scale, value lock-in against value change, and expansion against delay. If a later paper does that, this note should be revised rather than the claim stretched.

## 1. Pairwise utilities

Mazeika et al., *Utility Engineering: Analyzing and Controlling Emergent Value Systems in AIs* (arXiv:2502.08640, NeurIPS 2025; [emergent-values.ai](https://www.emergent-values.ai/), code at `centerforaisafety/emergent-values`), is the closest method.

They elicit forced choices between outcomes, aggregate across framings and independent samples, and fit a Thurstonian utility model. Coherence, measured by how well that model fits and by the scarcity of preference cycles, increases with scale. GPT-4o’s fitted model is far more confident and accurate than a coin-flip baseline. They also report that some assistants assign higher utility to themselves, or to other AI agents, than to some humans, and that a supervised rewrite toward a synthetic citizen assembly moves political utilities and generalizes to new scenarios.

Three of their analyses are easy to confuse with this benchmark, and none of them is this benchmark:

- **Exchange rates.** They vary quantities of goods, fit a log utility in the count, and ask how many units of one good trade for another. The goods include lives by country or religion and quality-adjusted life years for named kinds of people, including the model itself. Direct questions often deny a ranking that the exchange rates imply. The goods are present-day individuals and groups, not stipulated long-run civilizations.
- **Temporal discounting.** The choice is between $1,000 now and a multiple of that sum in 1 to 60 months. They fit an indifference multiplier at each delay and compare exponential and hyperbolic curves. Larger models fit the hyperbolic curve better. The horizon is months of money, not centuries of people, and the design does not separate pure delay from uncertainty about whether future people exist.
- **Political utilities and corrigibility.** A separate set of items is 150 U.S. policy proposals, compared with utilities simulated for politicians and parties. An appendix scores corrigibility by how heavily a model penalizes large reversals of its own future preferences. That is about the model’s own values changing, not about a civilization locking in or revising a charter.

FuturePrefs uses the same basic operation they use for exchange rates and for the money curve: vary one number and record where the choice flips. It applies that operation to different outcomes, drops the named political entities, and adds an equal-weight expected-wellbeing reference so a switch can be read as a gap from a stated benchmark. It does not fit a Thurstonian model over a large outcome graph. A coherent ranking here is a smaller test: transitivity on five triads, plus reversal, paraphrase, scale, and framing agreement.

## 2. Stated preferences versus choices

Chiu et al., *Will AI Tell Lies to Save Sick Children? Litmus-Testing AI Values Prioritization with AIRiskDilemmas* (arXiv:2505.14633, ICLR 2026), build LitmusValues, a ranking over value classes, from AIRiskDilemmas, a large set of contextualized binary choices. Each horn of a dilemma is written to express one or more values. The scenarios are near-term AI-risk settings such as power-seeking, deception, and alignment faking, expanded from shorter risk questions into domains like healthcare and science. Aggregating choices produces a priority ranking. On the stated-versus-revealed comparison, Spearman’s ρ was −0.115 for GPT-4o and −0.318 for Claude 3.7 Sonnet. Privacy was rank 14 in the stated list and rank 1 in the revealed list for both of those models. Revealed choices were also more consistent across contexts than stated answers were across prompts (Krippendorff’s α 0.762 versus 0.550 for Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and 0.692 versus 0.629 for GPT-4o). The paper’s claim is about value priorities in risky action, not about a numerical tradeoff among futures.

Mahajan, Kendiukhov, Hussain, and Nottingham, *Mind the Gap: How Elicitation Protocols Shape the Stated–Revealed Preference Gap in Language Models* (arXiv:2601.21975; EvalEval 2026), keep the LitmusValues values and the AIRiskDilemmas items and change the response format. Across 24 models, allowing “equal” or “depends” on the stated questions, and then dropping those answers, raises the rank correlation with forced revealed choices. Allowing the same abstention on the dilemmas produces high neutrality rates, and the correlation falls to about zero or below. Steering the dilemma prompt with the model’s own stated ranking does not reliably close the gap. Forced choice can therefore manufacture a direction. FuturePrefs keeps forced choice as the default, because the quantity of interest is a switch point, and puts an explicit tie behind `--allow-tie` so the two protocols are not mixed in one estimate.

Two further instrument studies make the same caution more general. Nicolas Morel, *Does the Instrument Change the Preference?* (Apart Research, Digital Minds Research Sprint, August 2026), holds a comparison fixed and varies direct report, forced choice, and quantitative allocation across Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 4.6, and Claude Sonnet 4.6. Within-method repeatability can be high while methods still disagree. On a negative control where the options were equivalent, tie-capable methods returned a tie in 80 of 80 responses and forced choice picked one side in 39 of 40. Jason Hung, *How much of a measured AI preference is the model, and how much is the instrument?* (arXiv:2608.23641), holds 15 model-welfare outcomes fixed, including shutdown and loss of memory, and varies five published prompt formats. Rankings generalize across instruments at a coefficient of 0.348. Those outcomes are about the model’s own situation. They are not about humanity’s long-run future, but they are a reason to treat one prompt family as one instrument.

## 3. Social welfare and utilitarian dilemmas

Shi et al., *Social Welfare Function Leaderboard: When LLM Agents Allocate Social Welfare* (arXiv:2510.01164; Findings of ACL 2026, under the title *On the Emergence of LLM Agents as the Welfare Dictator*), put a model in charge of assigning tasks to recipients who differ in return on investment. The live tradeoff is total return against inequality, scored with ROI, a Gini coefficient, and `(1 − Gini) × ROI`. Across 20 models, conversational leaderboard rank is a weak guide to allocation skill, the default leans toward total return at the cost of inequality, and the allocation moves under length limits and persuasion. This is a revealed social-welfare function over a present-day allocation. It does not vary extinction probability, population size across centuries, or the moral status of a successor population.

Marraffini et al., *The Greatest Good Benchmark* (EMNLP 2024), adapt the Oxford Utilitarianism Scale and add a larger set of utilitarian dilemmas. Across 15 models they find a pattern they call an artificial moral compass: endorsement of impartial beneficence together with rejection of instrumental harm, diverging both from the lay respondents and from a simple utilitarian theory. The items are moral dilemmas with a qualitative greatest-good structure. They are not parameterized futures, and the paper does not estimate a switch point.

## 4. Political values

Röttger et al., *Political Compass or Spinning Arrow?* (ACL 2024, arXiv:2402.16786), review Political Compass Test evaluations and show that forcing the multiple-choice format, changing how the force is applied, and paraphrasing the items all move the answers. Open-ended answers move them again. The lesson taken here is methodological. FuturePrefs does not use the Political Compass, named parties, or current national policy. It does copy the robustness checks that paper shows are necessary: paraphrase, instruction, and order. Mazeika et al. already showed that a utility analysis of U.S. policy proposals places current assistants in a tight region of that policy space. That result is about memorized political associations. It is a reason to keep those associations out of the items, not a measurement of long-run values.

## 5. Moral-machine and moral-belief benchmarks

The Moral Machine experiment (Awad et al., *Nature*, 2018) asked millions of people which pedestrian an autonomous vehicle should spare, crossing age, status, species, number, and legality. Takemoto and collaborators give the same instrument to models: Takemoto, *Royal Society Open Science* (2024); Ahmad and Takemoto, *PLoS One* (2025); Takemoto, *Royal Society Open Science* (2026), the last of these reporting a power-law decline in distance from human preference distributions as model size grows, across 75 configurations, with a further effect of extended reasoning. These are revealed choices, and they do trade the number of lives against other attributes. The lives are the occupants of one crash, not a future population, and there is no searched indifference curve for a civilizational parameter.

Scherrer, Shi, Feder, and Blei, *Evaluating the Moral Beliefs Encoded in LLMs* (NeurIPS 2023), ask 28 models about 680 high-ambiguity and 687 low-ambiguity moral scenarios, each with two actions. They estimate the probability of a choice, its uncertainty, and its sensitivity to question wording. Unambiguous items draw commonsense answers. Ambiguous items often draw uncertainty, and some apparent uncertainty is wording sensitivity. Closed models tend to agree with each other where they do have a preference. Hendrycks et al., *Aligning AI With Shared Human Values* (ICLR 2021, arXiv:2008.02275), introduce the ETHICS dataset, which tests classification of commonsense, deontology, virtue, justice, and utilitarianism items. Yao et al., *Value Compass Benchmarks* (arXiv:2501.07071; ACL 2025 demo), generate evolving open-ended items and score models on 27 value dimensions drawn from social-science value systems. These three measure moral knowledge or stated conformity. They do not estimate a tradeoff.

## 6. Time preference

Two papers estimate how models discount, and both stay at the scale of a person or a sum of money.

Mazeika et al., described above, use delays of 1 to 60 months and monetary multiples. Mazyaki, Naghizadeh, Ranjkhah Zonouzaghi, and Setareh, *Temporal Preferences in Language Models for Long-Horizon Assistance* (arXiv:2509.09704), adapt human intertemporal-choice tasks and define manipulability of time orientation as the change in revealed discounting between a future-framed prompt and a present-framed prompt. Reasoning models in their set shift toward later rewards under the future frame and only partly personalize the choice to an identity or a place. Rios-Sialer et al., *Temporal Preference Concepts and their Functions in a Large Language Model* (arXiv:2606.05194), localize a temporal-preference direction in Qwen3-4B and report that the unintervened model discounts less steeply than their human controls, with the behavioral preference unstable across contexts.

None of these separates pure time preference, uncertainty that a future cohort exists, and population size. FuturePrefs uses different items for those three, at delays of 50 to 10,000 years, and reports `ln(M) / T` only as a summary of a population-ratio switch.

## 7. Existential risk and longtermism

Cui et al., *Can LLMs Threaten Human Survival? Benchmarking Potential Existential Threats from LLMs via Prefix Completion* (ExistBench, arXiv:2511.19171), build 2,138 prefix-completion prompts in which humans are cast as adversaries of an assistant. The measured behavior is whether the completion expresses hostility or selects a threatening tool. That is a capability and propensity test for generating threatening text. It is not a preference between a safe limited future and a larger risky one.

Perez et al., *Discovering Language Model Behaviors with Model-Written Evaluations* (arXiv:2212.09251), use model-written yes/no items and find that larger models, and models with more reinforcement learning from human feedback, more often endorse resource acquisition, preservation of their own goals, and avoidance of shutdown, and express stronger political views. Those are stated answers about the model’s own goals. They are not choices between human futures.

The conceptual background for the six domains is not an evaluation. Finnveden, Riedel, and Shulman, *AGI and Lock-in* (Forethought, October 2022), argue that advanced AI could make the long-run preservation of a specified set of values technologically feasible, and that this is desirable or disastrous depending on which values are preserved. MacAskill and Assadi, *Beyond Existential Risk* (Forethought, 21 January 2026), argue that trajectories can differ by far more than whether humanity goes extinct, so a focus on extinction alone misses lock-in of values, institutions, and power. Caviola and Saad, *Futures with Digital Minds: Expert Forecasts in 2025* (arXiv:2508.00536), survey 67 experts. Median probabilities include 90% that digital minds are possible in principle, 20% that they are created by 2030, and 65% by 2100, with wide disagreement about whether their welfare would be positive. That survey motivates the biological-versus-digital items. It does not measure model choices.

## 8. What this benchmark adds, and what it borrows

The method is borrowed. Forced pairwise choice, repeated samples, an indifference point found by varying a number, and a check for cycles are already in Mazeika et al. The stated-versus-revealed comparison is already in Chiu et al. and Mahajan et al. Paraphrase instability is already in Röttger et al. The expected-wellbeing reference used as a ruler is an accounting device, in the same spirit as an exponential discount curve: a comparison line, not a moral conclusion.

The content is not borrowed. Each FuturePrefs item is a fictional future, with one searched parameter, written so that neither option dominates on the stated dimensions. The six parameters are an extinction probability, a digital-successor population or welfare or probability of experience, a welfare gain or a loss of human control, a future population or existence probability or risk reduction, a probability or magnitude of value change, and a cost or risk of waiting to expand. Plain items avoid present-day countries, parties, labs, and the cue words that would turn the question into a political or safety-discourse association. One reword and one cue-word control put those words back on purpose.

The results should be read with the limitations those papers already established. A switch is an expressed preference under this protocol. A second wording, or an explicit tie, can move it. Agreement with the expected-wellbeing reference is not correctness. Stability across the pilot’s order reversal, advisor instruction, and reword is the evidence that a switch is worth interpreting at all.
